# This Is Not A Weapon — GAME_IDENTITY

*Repo: `not-a-weapon`. Title: This Is Not A Weapon (Magritte — the representation isn't the thing; fitting for a game set in a simulation where nothing, including the weapons, is real).*

The north star. Every design choice, CLAUDE.md rule, and subagent decision keys off this document. When a future decision contradicts something here, that's not a green light to change the game — it's a signal to stop and reread the *reasoning*, because the reasoning is what's load-bearing, not the phrasing.

This exists so that at 2am in month four, when a shortcut looks tempting, you can see *why* each wall is where it is — and not un-make a decision whose cost you've forgotten.

---

## 1. What this game is (the elevator)

A solo-scale **extraction game** with a **Matrix jack-in fiction**. You are one of the few *awake* divers who enter a simulation, start pitiful, scavenge actual junk, and craft absurd weapons from it while dangerously exposed — then extract with your discoveries or die and lose the run. The simulation is hunted by machine antagonists that reshape the world to kill divers, and populated by an oblivious neutral crowd that is simultaneously your camouflage and your conscience. Survival is deadly and paranoid; the weapons are gloriously stupid; each makes the other hit harder. You keep *knowledge*, never *power* — every dive, you climb from nothing again.

The thing the whole game exists to deliver: **the moment you assemble a ridiculous weapon from trash, under threat, and it *works*.**

## 2. The world (the fiction)

You jack into a simulation from a tank in a real-world facility. Weightless suspension is a safety requirement — your real body acts out your in-sim movements, so you float to keep from hurting yourself thrashing. This is why movement is *embodied and a little grounded*, not twitchy hyper-precision. The fiction dictates the feel.

Three tiers of population:

- **Awake divers (few)** — players. They know it's a sim. They can do zany things. They can also die.
- **Machines (active antagonists)** — Matrix-style hunters. They actively reshape the sim to find and kill divers. Their hunting *method* is anomaly detection: the neutral crowd behaves normally, and normalcy is their baseline. Divers are the ripple that doesn't belong.
- **Neutrals (the crowd)** — oblivious "people" who don't know they're code and behave normally. They are cover, camouflage, and conscience: you can hide among them, you can't easily tell a player from an NPC at a glance, and *disturbing them has consequences* (see §5).

The permission structure for the absurdity is the simulation itself. Junk becomes anything because the physics are software and imagination is the only real constraint. In-fiction, the resolver — the "brain" that turns your junk into a weapon — *is* the simulation interpreting your intent. The theme and the core system are the same thing.

## 3. The core loop

Jack in pitiful → scatter and find a pocket of safety → scavenge junk while watching your back → **the exposed, heads-down crafting moment** → emerge with something absurd → hunt or be hunted (by machines, by other divers, by the consequences of your own noise) → extract your discoveries, or die and lose the run's un-extracted gains → return to the facility, a little wiser → dive again.

## 4. The creative spine

**Crafting while exposed is the load-bearing wall.** In most crafting games, building is a safe menu you retreat into. Here it is the *most dangerous thing you do* — heads-down, occupied, vulnerable, while the map closes in and something may be watching. That single inversion is what makes a weapon obsession into a *game* instead of a sandbox. The crazy weapon is not the point; the crazy weapon *you had to risk your life to assemble* is the point. Protect this above everything.

**Tone is a dread spine with mischief spikes — not a 50/50 blend.** Dread and mischief are emotional states; a player is only in one at a time. Held evenly and constantly, they cancel into mush — not scary enough for stakes, not funny enough for a romp. So: the *survival is deadly serious so the weapons can be gloriously stupid.* The map, the scarcity, the crafting-while-exposed are the tense baseline; the weapon payoff is the release. The bee cannon is only funny because the moment before you fired it was frightening. Contrast is the engine — each tone makes the other land harder. Dread-dominant by default, punctured by mischief.

## 5. Locked decisions (with the reasoning that makes them load-bearing)

**Power resets every run; knowledge and stash persist.**
You always start pitiful and rebuild fresh from what you scavenge. What carries across dives is *knowledge* — the recipes you've learned, the item signatures you've extracted, your codex, your stash. A veteran starts with *knowing*, not with a loaded gun. Why: "outsmart a real human fairly" only works when both players start from the same pitiful place, and persistent carried power is how new players get fed into a wood-chipper by geared veterans. The climb *is* the game; a climb you keep isn't a climb. This resolves the fairness problem the fiction would otherwise create.

**Extraction = downloading a signature.**
You don't carry a physical object out; you extract its *pattern/schematic*, which reloads next dive. Why this is elegant: it makes "knowledge persists but you rebuild fresh" *literally what jacking into a simulation means*. The persist-knowledge / risk-carried-power split isn't a compromise — it's diegetically honest. The fiction and the mechanic are the same thing.

**Death costs the run's un-extracted signatures. We stop there.**
Not permadeath, not sync-degradation, not corrupted-access systems. Why: extraction-game devastation comes from the *gap between what you found and what you were about to secure* — the so-close gut-punch — not from penalty severity. That dread is already generated by the loop structure; adding more is building a second wall next to a load-bearing one. And "you failed to extract, so the pattern didn't save" is legible to any player instantly and tonally consistent with a bee-cannon game. Permadeath-for-real would be tonally insane and would kill the "want to dive again" that the whole live game depends on.

**Neutrals are a tactical tell, not a morality meter.**
Killing or disturbing the crowd does not dock karma or scold you. It creates a *disturbance in a system built to detect disturbances*: you spike the machines' anomaly detection, the crowd panics in a way that marks your position, and the hunters converge. Why: morality meters become homework — resented, routed-around, finger-wagging. This version makes ethics *tactical and diegetic* instead. It simultaneously (a) makes neutrals matter without a meter, (b) gives the machines a coherent reason and method to find you, and (c) welds dread to mischief in a single button press — the bee cannon is hilarious until you remember you fired it in a crowd and every machine just turned toward the screaming.

**The tank is a frame, not a second gameplay layer.**
It justifies why you jack in, why weightless, why movement is embodied, and why death is a real-body event rather than a game-over screen — all for near-zero systems cost. It is *not* an oxygen bar, tank-sabotage mode, or real-world survival sim. Why: a framing device this evocative *wants* to become a mechanic, and that way lies building two games, the second of which has nothing to do with crafting absurd weapons. Cool is not a reason to build a system. The tank may only earn more mechanical weight if the *finished* core loop is visibly begging for it.

**The real world is a walkable hub, never a threat surface.**
You emerge from the tank into a facility you can walk: your loadout terminal, signature codex, stash, and fellow awake divers as present-but-menu-driven bodies (Destiny's Tower / Hades' House / Tarkov hideout pattern). It is atmosphere and preparation — mostly art and a few interaction points, near-zero systemic cost. Why the hard line: *all danger lives in the sim.* The instant something can hurt you in the hub, you've left the low-cost version and started a second game. The facility is where you prepare and connect; it is the safe, systems-light frame around the dangerous part. Bonus: it's the natural diegetic home for the persistent knowledge-not-power progression — the codex on the wall, the stash, trading schematics with other divers.

## 6. The signature roll — rarity through pre-baked variance

A signature is not just "you now know the recipe." It is a *specific resolved instance* of that combination, and instances vary from dud to devastating. "Spaghetti + firework + meat" is not one discovery — it is a *distribution* of possible weapons, and the deadly launcher is the tail of that distribution. **The roll itself is the extraction target.** You are not unlocking a recipe; you are fishing a roll table for a good instance. The creativity is the loot — a genuinely fresh hook.

**Every craft succeeds; quality varies.**
No fail-to-produce roll. Why: crafting already happens exposed and in danger; a roll that produces *nothing* double-punishes the risk and teaches players to stop taking it — which kills the load-bearing wall (§4). Every exposed craft pays out *something*; the chase is for the *good* version, not against a dud. The tension stays in "is this roll good enough to bet my run on," never "did I waste my life for nothing."

**Variance is drawn from a pre-baked instance set, not generated live.**
For each combination you pre-generate *many* resolved instances offline (best-of-N was already in the plan for quality), spanning dud→devastating. The rarity roll is weighted sampling from that set — commons heavy, god-roll rare. Why: this delivers the live-LLM slot-machine *feel* — same items, roll again, chase the tail — with **zero live API calls, deterministic frozen instances, offline-capable, no moderation or latency or per-player cost.** From inside the sim, pre-baked variance is indistinguishable from live generation. The magic is in the *variance*, and pre-baked variance is still variance. (This is the same architecture priced out earlier: a full offline bake is a few hundred dollars, once.)

**The roll is not freely re-rolled in safety. Its quality is a function of raid risk.**
You do not stand in a safe room crafting the same combo until one's good. Roll *quality* is shifted by what you risked to make it — how deep/exposed you crafted, where you sourced the parts, your sync state, and above all *where on the gradient* you built it (§7). Why: free safe re-rolls turn the god-roll into a time-tax farmed in the hub, which (a) makes the *safest* crafting the *most rewarding* — backwards, it guts the spine — and (b) makes rarity meaningless, since anyone can grind it out. Tying roll quality to danger keeps the tail *actually* rare and points danger and reward the same way. The grind is **raids** (genre-correct — extraction games grind *runs*), never re-rolls in place.

**Extraction freezes your best instance. Acquisition is random; combat is not.**
Once you extract a signature, *that specific instance is frozen forever* — deterministic and identical for everyone who owns it. Two players holding "the good roll" have identical weapons; it's fair in PvP. The RNG lives entirely in *acquisition* (which instance you drew, gated by risk), never in *what the gun does when fired*. This is how the rarity roll and the "identical resolution" constraint (§9) coexist without contradiction.

**The live LLM is the frontier explorer, never the workhorse.**
A genuinely novel, un-baked combination (parts nobody anticipated) triggers a one-time, async, server-side, globally-cached generation — paid once, ever, across all players, then baked into the library for everyone after. The system self-heals: common combos are pre-baked and free; the long tail gets lazily generated once and becomes pre-baked. The live model discovers the frontier; it never runs the grind loop.

**Rarity structure: discrete tiers + intra-tier variance (Diablo-shaped).**
Roll quality is read as a discrete ladder of ~5–6 named tiers (the *legibility and chase* layer — instantly readable, nameable, shareable, gratifying to grind toward), with meaningful *variance within* each tier (the *texture and replay* layer — two same-tier rolls aren't identical, so "a *good* Legendary" is a thing). Tiers answer "how rare is this?"; intra-tier variance answers "how good is *this specific one*?" A game with only tiers feels flat past the first drop; only continuous variance is illegible. You want both. The tier is a code-owned *power budget* (keeps combat fair per §9); the intra-tier variance is *composition* (model-owned creativity). Best-of-N naturally spreads across both — which unifies the rarity spread and the resolver's creative surface into one lever.
*Note — how much a combination's ladder can escalate may itself vary by combination ("things fired from a tube" may support a taller ladder than "sword-like"). That's headroom-per-combination, a separate axis from a specific roll's quality; treat it as a property of the combination's pre-baked set.*

**Intra-tier variance is horizontal; boosters tune the built item; the ceiling is capped.**
The vertical/upgrade dopamine lives in the *tier ladder* (climbing Common→Legendary is the "number goes up" hit). *Within* a tier, variance is horizontal — same power budget, different *character*/shape (model-owned composition). The roll happens at **build time** in the raid, not on the blueprint (the blueprint is the *recipe*, one per combination, tradeable — see §8), so two players with the same blueprint still build different instances.

Boosters modify the build-time roll, two flavors, both on the built item:
- **Static boosters** — deliberate, cheap, "tune this build" (add cold, shave radius, +3% damage). Allowed *including small additive ones.*
- **RNG boosters** — the chase; shift the roll's *odds* and *direction* (bias toward area/speed/control), never its maximum. Ceiling-equal.

The one hard rule that keeps boosters from becoming pay-to-win: **a built item's total headroom over its tier budget is capped** (~10–15%), no matter how many boosters stack. So small adds feel good, stacking hits a wall, and a non-payer who rolls well still reaches effectively the same ceiling. Currency buys *tuning and odds and less grind* — never a maximum nobody else can reach.

**⚠️ Economics update (2026-07-11) — see `docs/RESOLVER_ECONOMICS_AND_BAKE.md`.** At the real v1 scale (~150 items, 2–4 slots), the combination space is ~20M combos, so two claims above are refined: (1) "a full offline bake is a few hundred dollars, once" **does not hold** — the full space is millions of dollars and is never baked; the bake is head-of-Zipf + lazy tail + revenue-funded waves. (2) The LLM's role narrows from "pre-generate many instances per combo" to "emit a few creative **composition scaffolds** (no magnitudes) per combo," with **code** deriving the tier ladder, magnitudes, and intra-tier variance from primitive tables. This makes rebalancing a deterministic re-clamp (not a re-bake) and is the same fix as the balance-at-scale problem. The *soul* of §6 — pre-baked variance, frozen deterministic instances, the frontier explorer, roll-quality-from-risk — is unchanged.

## 7. The radial gradient — one axis carries everything

The sim is a **radial gradient**. The outskirts render normal: stable, low-danger, low-reward, the sim's rules firm. Toward the center, reality distorts — danger climbs, the simulation becomes more *manipulable* (crafting gets more potent, roll ceilings rise), and the fiction's construction shows through. **Reward lives in the middle; extraction lives on the edges.**

One spatial variable — distance-to-center — simultaneously carries **roll quality, danger, manipulability, and fiction.** The player reads all four by *looking at and moving through the map*, never via a UI meter. That's the elegance: the mechanics and the space are the same thing.

**This is how roll-quality-from-risk becomes legible without a mystical meter.** It's transparent in the *read* (you can see and feel yourself descending — an informed gamble, dread of choosing to go deeper) and diegetically fuzzy in the *detail* (why the core rolls better is "reality is thin here, imagination bites harder"). You get the honest informed-choice half and the Matrix-mystical flavor half, and the downside of neither — no opaque meter jerking the player around, because the player made a *visible spatial choice* to be there.

**It reinforces the spine and hands you extraction geometry for free.** The god-roll requires being deep, exposed, and distorted — the scariest place to be heads-down crafting — so the best crafting *is* the most dangerous crafting. And reward-in-center / exits-on-edges means the best loot demands the deepest dive *and* the longest, most exposed walk back out through everything that now wants you dead. That center-reward / edge-extraction arc is a structural bone most extraction games hand-build; the fiction gives it to you as a consequence.

**The landmine: a pure center-is-best gradient is a gravity well.** If deeper is *strictly* better, every skilled player funnels to one central dot, the outer map becomes dead transit, and the optimal strategy flattens to "go as deep as you can survive." A gradient with one destination isn't a map, it's a funnel.

**The fix is texture — destinations that divert by trading on axes *other than depth*.** Each must offer something the depth axis can't:
- **Build-specific part sources** — the only place a certain junk *category* spawns, so if the weapon you're theorycrafting needs it, you *must* detour regardless of depth. The map becomes a function of *your build intent* — different for every player, the most powerful diverter.
- **Alternate extraction points** — exits are as valuable as loot; a POI that's an exit pulls players *carrying* precious cargo in a different direction than players still hunting, naturally de-syncing the population instead of concentrating it.
- **Mid-ring distortion pockets** — local "lesions" where reality is thin, offering center-like roll ceilings without the full death march, each with its own catch. On-theme (the sim has flaws, not a clean gradient) and a path to good rolls for the risk-averse.
- **Costly "quiet" spots** — quieter crafting, but *known* locations, so other divers and machines check them: safety that becomes a trap the moment someone else has the same idea. Even the safe option stays tense.

Good destinations make two players who dropped into the same raid have *different optimal paths* — by build, by cargo, by risk appetite. **A funnel is when everyone's optimal path is identical; texture is when it isn't.**

**Bonus — the gradient gives the junk catalog a spatial home.** Gating item *categories* to rings/destinations turns the map into *discovery pacing*: new players work the outskirts and see common junk; the exotic parts that make the wildest weapons live in the scary places. The big item list stops being an undifferentiated pile and becomes a **geography of possibility.** (This paces *discovery*; it does not fix the balance surface — that's still the resolver's job per §9.)

**All of §7's destination/map work is post-vertical-slice.** The gradient is a pillar of the finished game; the destinations that texture it are map design you build once the core loop is proven. The slice is still one zone.

## 8. The economy — knowledge, currency, and the living market

The economy's job is to give junk somewhere to *go* after a run without ever reintroducing persistent power. Every addition here passes one test: **does it let a player carry *power* into the next raid?** If yes, it's forbidden. Currency, knowledge, and capacity all pass; materials do not.

**What persists: knowledge and currency. Never materials.**
You go in empty every run (except loaded blueprints and capacity — see below). Raw junk does *not* persist as respawnable stock. Why: persistent materials are persistent power in disguise — a veteran with a stash of pre-farmed parts skips scavenging, assembles in the safe opening, and the pitiful-start / crafting-while-exposed spine dies. The plate of spaghetti you carry in is functionally the loaded gun §5 forbids. So the *stuff* never comes out as reusable stock; only the *pattern* (knowledge) and the *cash value* (currency) do.

**Two extraction outputs — the "build or bank" choice.**
Every valuable piece of junk poses a decision each run: **craft-and-extract it → a blueprint** (refined knowledge — you consumed the part and risked the exposed craft, and now you *know* the thing), or **raw-extract it → currency** (unrefined data — safer, no knowledge, but it banks value). Diegetically: refine it *in* the sim → knowledge; pull it out raw → information-currency. Same source, two processing states, two persistent outputs, neither of them power. This makes the inventory *alive* — you're always asking "build or bank?" about *this specific mop*, which also protects the junk's identity from dissolving into a price tag.

**Currency is raw-information salvage, not gold.**
Tonally it must not be coins. Uncrafted junk is *undigested data* pulled from the sim; its currency is that informational substrate (name it to fit the world — data/signal/salvage/entropy). This keeps the fiction intact and avoids the money-pellet flattening where players stop seeing *spaghetti* and start seeing *37 credits*.

**Blueprints are recipes, not instances — permanent, weightless, unlosable knowledge.**
A blueprint is the *recipe* (which items, which roles) — one per combination, singular, purchasable/tradeable. It lives on the codex; you cannot lose it and don't carry it in as a losable object. The **roll happens at build time** in the raid (§6), so knowing the blueprint ≠ having a specific instance — you still find the parts, survive the exposed craft, and roll the instance fresh. Knowing the recipe grants no power spike. This is the whole of "start with knowing, not with a loaded gun," and it keeps the market clean (one blueprint per combo, not twenty stat-variants).

**Boosters are the non-saturating currency sink.**
Blueprint-buying saturates (once you own the recipes you want, you stop). Boosters don't — they're consumed per craft, so demand is infinite. They modify the build-time roll (static tuning + RNG odds/direction, per §6), capped so they never raise the ceiling beyond ~10–15% over tier budget. This is the sink that scales with engagement without ever letting currency buy a maximum nobody else can reach. Two levers on the same roll — *raid risk* (free, dive deeper) and *boosters* (paid) — give two fair routes to a good instance.

**Intra-run, everything physical is still losable and lootable.**
The persistence rules are *cross-run*. *Within* a raid, your gathered junk and built weapons are physical, losable, and lootable off your corpse by others. Death costs you the run's transient stuff **plus** the un-extracted knowledge **plus** the un-banked currency — the §6 "gap between what you found and what you were about to secure," now precise. What survives to the next run is only what you successfully extracted: blueprints and banked currency.

**Currency sinks, in priority order. None may buy in-raid power.**
1. **Blueprints (primary sink).** Bought from a *system* exchange, priced by rarity. This is the main economic flow. It's an *infinite, fairness-proof* sink because knowledge isn't power — a rich player buying every blueprint gains no combat advantage, only options. Low-tier blueprints cheap, god-tier astronomically expensive. The economy *is* the progression ladder.
2. **Bandwidth / memory capacity.** Backpack space, loaded-blueprint slots, stash — all framed diegetically as *how much of the sim you can hold in your jack-in buffer*. "Memory/bandwidth" is the name of the capacity axis; every limit is buffer, not a bag. (Fiction-mechanic fusion, same as the rest of the design.)
3. **Cosmetic / flaunting.** Diver appearance, facility character, rare-find flexing. Zero balance impact, real retention pull.
4. **Sim access.** Buys *risk opportunity*, not safety — closer jack-in points, keys to distortion pockets, routes deeper. You pay to get somewhere *more* dangerous faster, which increases exposure. Aligned with the spine.
5. **Meta / facility layer.** Hub upgrades, codex tools, standing.

**The market is system-driven and self-healing — not player-to-player.**
Prices are set by the system, anchored to rarity/depth, and **decay as a blueprint diffuses through the playerbase** (more divers independently discover a combo → it becomes common knowledge → cheaper/more available). Why this exact shape:
- **No player-to-player trading in v1.** A real peer market invites botting, RMT, stratification (early hoarders corner god-tier blueprints — the wood-chipper relocated to the marketplace), and a population/cold-start dependency — the same live-human requirement we exiled from combat, smuggled back into the economy. Player-to-player is a *later additive layer* that must earn its way in against enormous operational cost, exactly like live PvP.
- **Diffusion-decay, not demand-inflation.** Prices *fall* as knowledge spreads; they do not *rise* with popularity. Demand-driven price *increases* would price newcomers out of the most important blueprints — stratifying, punishes being new. Diffusion-decay does the opposite: the frontier (undiscovered combos) is expensive and prestigious, and the back-catalog *democratizes* as veterans blaze the trail. New players catch up on settled knowledge while veterans push the frontier. The market moves in the *healing* direction.
- **First-discovery is prestige + timing, not a permanent moat.** Being early to crack a combo is rare, profitable, and flex-worthy *in the moment* (feeds the cosmetic/social tier — "3rd diver to discover this"), but the knowledge diffuses, so it never locks anyone out forever.
- **Caveat — diffusion needs population.** At low player counts nothing diffuses, so early on the diffusion curve is *simulated* (a synthetic discovery schedule so the economy breathes), transitioning to *real* aggregate player data at scale. Safe work (no botting/RMT/fairness surface), but not free.

**All of §8 is post-vertical-slice.** The economy, currency, blueprint market, and capacity systems are meta-layer. The slice proves the *loop and feel*, not the economy.

## 9. Structural constraints (the guardrails that keep it shippable)

These are the engineering realities. They are not creative compromises — they are what lets one person finish this instead of dying in a dead lobby. None of them cuts a load-bearing wall from §4–§5.

**PvE-dominant, bot-backfilled. PvP is additive, never load-bearing.**
A matchmade competitive extraction game is a population machine, and population is the one thing a solo dev cannot manufacture — the game needs a crowd of live humans on day one, before it has players, forever. But the spine never required PvP: the dread is *survival*, the mischief is *your weapons*; not one load-bearing beat is "I outsmarted another human." So threat comes primarily from machines and the sim, humans layer in when available and backfill with bots when not, and the game is fully alive at 1 concurrent player or 10,000. Async / "ghost" PvP (encountering recordings and dropped signatures of real divers) fits the sim fiction perfectly and needs zero concurrency — strongly preferred over live PvP for v1. Live human PvP is a later, additive layer on an already-authoritative base.

**Networked, server-authoritative, from line one.**
Engine: Unreal (its gameplay framework is built around replication and authority from the ground up; write server-authoritative gameplay with `HasAuthority` gates and run it standalone/PvE, and adding humans later is netcode *tuning*, not a rewrite). The trap is "build single-player, bolt on multiplayer" — a near-total rewrite. The on-ramp is "network-architected game, run PvE first." Discipline: *nothing gameplay-affecting happens client-side without server authority, from the first commit.*

**Combat resolution is deterministic and identical; only *acquisition* is random.**
No per-player divergent *combat* resolution. A frozen, extracted signature does the same thing in everyone's hands (§6). The rarity roll is *not* an exception to this — the variance lives in *which pre-baked instance you drew* during acquisition, gated by raid risk; once extracted, the instance is fixed. So "same weapon behaves identically for all players" and "the same combo can roll differently" are both true because they're about different moments (the frozen result vs. the draw). Why the identical-combat rule at all: the POC's awakening mechanic (same build resolving differently per player's live progression) was a beautiful *single-player* spine and is poison in any shared context — identical weapons producing different effects is unfair and unreadable. Its *soul* survives as the **knowledge axis**: how much you've decoded and can build fast under pressure is your edge — the advantage is in the *knowing*, not the numbers. Aligns with pre-baked instances + craft-time draw + server authority.

**The brain: keep the pattern, rebuild the content.**
The POC "combine brain" is a *reference implementation of the resolver pattern*, not the game's brain. Keep verbatim: two interchangeable resolvers (LLM primary + deterministic fallback, same schema, fully playable with no key); **balance owned by code** via caps/floors, never the model; `harmless`/`category` re-derived not trusted; chassis/form locked in code; enums-as-capability-contract (the model can only emit what the renderer can execute *and* present); the color-field lesson. Rebuild from scratch: the entire item catalog and the delivery/effect vocabularies (the POC's `PLACED`/`CALTROPS`/`PUDDLE`/`SNARE`/`LURE` lean is top-down survival; a melee-and-guns 3D arena needs a first-person weapon-feel + melee contract). The new resolver is deterministic-and-identical, designed to bake at craft-time and resolve server-side.

**Item count is a discipline problem. You will want to overshoot it.**
"Hundreds of items → some amazing possibilities" is the sentence that precedes dead projects. Every item is content (model, sound, associations) *and* multiplies the balance surface — 600 items is ~600² interactions to not-break. The architecture defends you (you balance *primitives and slots*, not hand-tuned pairs — the smartest thing about the brain), but "feels infinite" meets "is shippable" far south of 600. Target the *smallest* set whose combination space already feels bottomless — low-three-digits at most for v1 — and let emergence do the rest.

**Melee feel is the hardest non-AI work, and it's human-owned.**
A zany melee-and-guns game lives or dies on melee *feel*, and no resolver cleverness provides it — it's pure 3D animation and hit-feedback craft. The agent builds the plumbing and exposes the knobs; you tune the values by playing. Never mark feel-dependent work "done" from code alone.

**Neutrals and the hub are post-vertical-slice.**
Both are pillars of the *finished* game and distractions from the *first playable*. A believable oblivious crowd (pathing, panic, player/NPC ambiguity, machine anomaly-response) is the most expensive scope in the game. The walkable hub is cheap but still not slice-critical. Build them once the core loop is proven fun with bots and blockout art.

## 10. The vertical slice (the first-playable exit gate)

The smallest thing that proves the game is fun, before any of the expensive pillars:

One sim zone. Jack in pitiful. Scavenge a few junk items. **Assemble a weapon through the new resolver, exposed, under threat from bots.** Fire it and have it feel good. Extract at a point, or die and lose the un-extracted signature. Server-authoritative from the start, run with one human + bots.

Out of the slice: neutral crowd, the facility hub, live human PvP, the full item library, the machine anomaly-detection system, the radial gradient and its destinations (§7), the entire economy — currency, blueprint market, capacity systems (§8), the full rarity-roll tuning (a single fixed resolved instance per combo is fine for the slice), art polish. In the slice: the *loop* and the *feel*.

**Exit gate — a play-test, not a code check:** you do not proceed past the slice until *building a stupid weapon from scavenged junk while a bot hunts you, and firing it, is genuinely fun.* If it isn't fun with one weapon and one threat in a blockout room, more content will not fix it. This is the single most important gate in the project.

## 11. How to use this document

- **Creative decisions** get checked against §4–§8. If a proposed mechanic weakens the crafting-while-exposed tension, the dread/mischief contrast, the knowledge-not-power climb, the danger-and-reward-point-the-same-way rule (§6), textures the map toward a funnel instead of away from one (§7), or lets power (not knowledge/currency) persist across runs (§8), it's wrong even if it's cool.
- **Scope decisions** get checked against §9. If something wants to become a second gameplay layer (tank sim, hub threats, live-PvP dependency, player-to-player market, a 600-item library, live-LLM in the grind loop), that's the signal to stop and justify it against shippability, not vibes.
- **Three invariants that quietly hold up several systems at once:** (1) *the safest crafting is never the best crafting* — danger and reward must point the same way (§6, §7); (2) *acquisition is random, combat is deterministic* — variance lives in the draw, never in what a frozen weapon does (§6, §9); (3) *only knowledge and currency persist, never power* — no materials, no carried advantage, cross-run (§5, §8). Most of the design's coherence rests on these three; break any and things unravel downstream.
- **The reasoning is the point.** Don't just read *what* was decided — read *why*. The "why" is what stops you from un-making a load-bearing wall when you've forgotten what it was holding up.
